Fast estimates catch most RF design mistakes before a simulator does. This note collects the essentials in one place: the dB arithmetic and power levels you use every day, noise figure worked through one receiver chain, and the numbers worth memorizing for wavelength, antennas, cables, noise, and propagation, each with the formula behind it.

2Quick dB arithmetic

A ratio in decibels is defined on power, so a voltage (or current) ratio picks up a factor of two:

GdB=10log10⁡P2P1=20log10⁡V2V1(1)

Most link-budget mistakes are caught by a quick mental check. These ratios cover nearly every case:

dBPower, P2/P1Voltage, V2/V1
−30.5×0.71×
11.26×1.12×
32×1.41×
64×2×
1010×3.16×
20100×10×

Build anything else from these, since adding dB multiplies ratios:

  • 13 dB = 10 dB + 3 dB → 10 × 2 = 20× in power
  • 7 dB = 10 dB − 3 dB → 10 ÷ 2 = 5× in power
  • 26 dB = 20 dB + 6 dB → 100 × 4 = 400× in power, 10 × 2 = 20× in voltage

Pitfall

Know whether you're in power or voltage. A 6 dB gain doubles the voltage but quadruples the power. In optical links, photocurrent goes as optical power (I∝Popt) while RF power goes as current squared (PRF∝I2). So a 1 dB change in optical power becomes a 2 dB change in RF power after the photodiode.

4What noise figure measures

The noise factor F is how much a block degrades the signal-to-noise ratio, measured with a source at the standard temperature T0=290 K:

F=SNRinSNRout,NF=10log10⁡FdB(2)
  • A perfect, noiseless block has F=1, so NF=0 dB.
  • NF=3 dB (F≈2): the block adds as much noise as the source already had.
  • F is the linear ratio and NF is the same thing in decibels. The math uses F and datasheets use NF, so you convert back and forth constantly.

Below about 1 dB, it is easier to compare stages by their equivalent noise temperature:

Te=T0(F−1)⇔F=1+TeT0(3)

Tip

Think in kelvin for quiet stages. A 0.5 dB amplifier has F≈1.122, so Te≈290×0.122≈35 K. A 0.7 dB part is about 51 K. The 0.2 dB difference becomes a 45% difference in added noise.

Pitfall

Never add noise figures in dB. Convert NF and gain to linear ratios first, apply Friis (eq. 4), then convert the total back to dB.

5The Friis formula

For n blocks in cascade, each with noise factor Fi and available gain Gi, both linear:

Ftot=F1+F2−1G1+F3−1G1G2+⋯=F1+∑i=2nFi−1∏j=1i−1Gj(4)

Read it as: each stage's added noise, Fi−1, is divided by all the gain in front of it. Once the signal has been amplified, later stages have a much harder time hurting it. The same formula in noise temperature is Ttot=T1+T2/G1+⋯.

6One receiver chain, worked through

Take a simple three-stage receiver: a low-noise amplifier (LNA) → mixer → IF amplifier.

2 · Mixer10 dB8 dB10.0100 (20 dB)9.0 ÷ 100 = 0.090

Total1.78 dB1.505
Ftot=1.413+0.090+0.002=1.505⇒NFtot=10log10⁡1.505≈1.78 dB
  • The mixer's 10 dB noise figure looks terrible on its own. Behind 20 dB of LNA gain it adds only ≈ 0.3 dB to the total.
  • The IF amplifier barely registers.
  • The receiver's noise figure ≈ the LNA's noise figure + a small penalty. In kelvin, the whole chain is Te≈290×0.505≈146 K.

7Where a cable belongs

A passive, lossy block at T0 has a noise factor equal to its loss, and a gain equal to its inverse:

F=L,G=1L⇒NF=LdB(5)

Add 2 dB of cable or filter loss (L≈1.585) to the chain above:

Where the 2 dB loss goesFtotNFtotPenalty
Nowhere (baseline)1.5051.78 dB—
Before the LNA1.585 × 1.505 = 2.3853.78 dB+2.00 dB
After the LNA1.5651.94 dB+0.17 dB
  • Before the LNA, the loss adds dB for dB: nothing is in front of it to divide it down.
  • After the LNA, the cable's own term is only 0.585 ÷ 100 ≈ 0.006. Most of the 0.17 dB comes from the mixer, which now sees 18 dB of gain in front of it, not 20 dB.

This is why antennas often have the LNA mounted right at the feed, and why the input filter's loss is fought over so hard.

8From noise figure to sensitivity

The thermal noise power density from a matched source at T0 is:

kT0=(1.38×10−23)×290≈4.0×10−21 W/Hz≈−174 dBm/Hz(6)

A receiver's sensitivity, the weakest signal it can detect, follows directly:

Pmin=−174 dBm/Hz⏟noise floor+10log10⁡B⏟bandwidth+NF⏟receiver+SNRmin⏟demodulator(7)

For our chain with B=20 MHz and a demodulator that needs SNRmin=10 dB:

Pmin=−174+73+1.78+10≈−89 dBm

Every dB saved in the LNA, or in the loss in front of it, is a dB of sensitivity. Bandwidth terms worth remembering:

B1 MHz20 MHz100 MHz1 GHz
10log10⁡B60 dB73 dB80 dB90 dB
Noise floor −174+10log10⁡B−114 dBm−101 dBm−94 dBm−84 dBm

Tip

Use eq. (7) to check a datasheet claim. A 100 MHz receiver claims −100 dBm sensitivity with 10 dB SNR. The floor plus SNR alone is −174 + 80 + 10 = −84 dBm, before any noise figure. Reaching −100 dBm would need NF=−16 dB, which is impossible since F≥1. Something in the claim is off.

Pitfall

Check whether a mixer NF is SSB or DSB. A double-sideband figure is about 3 dB lower than the single-sideband figure for the same mixer. Mixing them up hides 3 dB in your budget.

Tip

kT/C does not depend on the resistor. A larger switch resistance makes the noise denser but limits its bandwidth by the same factor, so the total sampled noise is set by the capacitor alone. To halve the noise voltage in a sampling circuit you need four times the capacitance.

10Frequency and wavelength

A wave travels one wavelength in one period. In free space it moves at the speed of light, so

λ=cf⇒λm≈300fMHz(8)

The second form is the one to memorize: divide 300 by the frequency in MHz and you get the wavelength in metres.

  • 15 MHz → 300 ÷ 15 = 20 m. This is the example worth remembering: a 15 MHz wave is 20 m long.
  • 300 MHz → exactly 1 m. 3 GHz → 10 cm. 30 GHz → 1 cm. 300 GHz → 1 mm.
  • Each factor of 10 in frequency is a factor of 10 in wavelength, so these four anchors give you any other value by scaling.

Tip

Amateur radio bands are named by wavelength. The “20 m band” is 14.000–14.350 MHz, where 300 ÷ 14.2 ≈ 21 m. The “40 m band” is near 7 MHz and the “2 m band” near 144 MHz. If someone gives you a length in metres for a band, they mean the wavelength, not the antenna.

11Antenna lengths

The simplest resonant antenna, the half-wave dipole, is two wires fed in the middle with a total length of about half a wavelength. A quarter-wave monopole is one of those halves standing over a ground plane, which acts as a mirror for the other half. Real wires have thickness and their ends have fringing capacitance, so resonance happens about 5% short of the ideal length:

Pitfall

“A 20 m antenna” is ambiguous. A wave at 15 MHz is 20 m long, but a wire 20 m long, cut as a half-wave dipole, resonates near 143 ÷ 20 ≈ 7.1 MHz, and as a quarter-wave vertical near 71 ÷ 20 ≈ 3.6 MHz. For 15 MHz you want a dipole about 9.5 m long. Always ask whether a length is the wavelength or the antenna.

Numbers that come with the dipole:

  • Input resistance ≈ 73 Ω for an ideal thin half-wave dipole, about 70 Ω at the slightly shorter resonant length, which is why 75 Ω coax suits it well.
  • Gain 2.15 dBi. “dBd” is gain relative to a dipole, so 0 dBd = 2.15 dBi.
  • A quarter-wave monopole over a large ground plane has half the resistance, about 36 Ω, and 3 dB more gain, 5.15 dBi, because it radiates only into the upper half-space.
  • An antenna much smaller than λ/10 is “electrically small”: its radiation resistance drops roughly as the square of its size, so ohmic loss and matching loss take over.

In a cable or on a circuit board the wave travels slower, by the square root of the effective dielectric constant, so the wavelength shrinks by the same factor:

Two rules decide when a wire must be treated as a transmission line, with a controlled impedance and a termination:

  • Analog signals: when the length exceeds about λ/10 at the highest frequency of interest. At 2.45 GHz on FR-4 microstrip that is only about 7 mm.
  • Digital edges: the useful bandwidth of an edge is about 0.35/tr (10–90% rise time). A 35 ps edge reaches 10 GHz even if the data rate is far lower.

13Metals and free space

At high frequency, current crowds into a thin layer at the surface of a conductor, the skin depth:

δ=ρπfμ0δCu≈66 µmfMHz(11)
  • Copper: 66 µm at 1 MHz, 6.6 µm at 100 MHz, 2.1 µm at 1 GHz, 0.66 µm at 10 GHz.
  • A conductor more than about three skin depths thick gains nothing more at that frequency; surface roughness comparable to δ adds loss.
  • Free space has a wave impedance of μ0/ε0≈377 Ω.

14How far a signal goes

Between two isotropic antennas in free space, the loss grows with the square of both distance and frequency (Friis):

FSPLdB=20log10⁡dkm+20log10⁡fMHz+32.45(12)
  • 2.4 GHz over 1 m: 40 dB; over 100 m: 80 dB.
  • Doubling the distance costs 6 dB; ten times the distance costs 20 dB.
  • The frequency term reflects a smaller antenna aperture at higher frequency, not extra loss in the air. With fixed-size dish antennas at both ends, higher frequency actually gains.

Questions & answers

Why is noise figure defined with a 290 K source?

It is a convention, chosen as roughly room temperature, so that numbers from different labs and datasheets compare directly (k·290 K ≈ 4.0 × 10−21 W/Hz, or −174 dBm/Hz). When the real source is much colder, like an antenna looking at the sky, work in noise temperature instead: the noise figure alone no longer tells you the SNR.

Can a noise figure be negative?

Not for a linear two-port fed from a 290 K source: F ≥ 1, so NF ≥ 0 dB. A negative number on a datasheet means a different definition (for example a cooled reference) or a measurement error.

Why does loss in front of the LNA count dB for dB?

A passive, lossy block at 290 K has F = L (eq. 5), and as the first stage its noise is not divided by any gain. Try it in the receiver budget above: move 2 dB of loss before and after the LNA.

If an antenna is 20 m long, does it work at 15 MHz?

Not as a resonant antenna. At 15 MHz the wavelength is 20 m. A 20 m wire is a full wavelength at 15 MHz, and fed in the middle it has a very high impedance that needs a tuner. As a half-wave dipole, a 20 m wire resonates near 7.1 MHz. For 15 MHz, cut a dipole of about 9.5 m, or a quarter-wave vertical of about 4.7 m.

Why is the amateur “20 m band” at 14 MHz and not at 15 MHz?

The names are rounded wavelengths, not exact ones. 300 ÷ 14.2 MHz ≈ 21 m, which rounds to “20 m”. A broadcaster at 15 MHz really is at 20 m.

Why are real dipoles about 5% shorter than half a wavelength?

A wire of finite thickness has fringing capacitance at its ends, so the current distribution behaves as if the wire were slightly longer. The input reactance crosses zero at about 0.47–0.48 λ, not at 0.5 λ. Thicker wires need more shortening.

When should I use the wavelength in the material and when the free-space one?

Use the free-space wavelength for antennas that radiate into air, and the guided wavelength from eq. (10) for transmission lines, stubs, and filters on boards or chips. Antennas printed on a substrate fall in between: they shrink by roughly the square root of an effective dielectric constant between 1 and that of the substrate.

How is noise figure measured on a real chip?

Most often with the Y-factor method: a calibrated noise source is switched on and off and the ratio of output powers gives F. The note on post-silicon measurement walks through it with a calculator.
